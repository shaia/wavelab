# The microscopic origin of pressure

(04-pressure-puzzle)=
## The puzzle: violence underneath, calm on top

Press your palm against a table and the force you feel is steady. Now press it against the
air — the atmosphere pushes back on every square centimetre of your skin with about ten
newtons, and that push is steady too. Steady enough that a barometer built in 1643 still
works today.

But the air is not a smooth fluid pressing on you. It is roughly $3 \times 10^{19}$ molecules
per cubic centimetre, each moving at hundreds of metres per second, each striking your skin,
rebounding, and flying off. Nothing about a single collision is gentle or regular. A molecule
arrives at a random moment, from a random direction, with a random speed.

So here is the puzzle this module exists to resolve:

:::{important} The question
Every individual event is violent, random and discrete. How does their sum become a number
so steady that we can build instruments on it — and treat it as a *property of the gas*?
:::

The answer will turn out to have nothing to do with the collisions becoming gentler. It has
to do with *how many* of them there are, and it will give us our first quantitative law of
large numbers: the relative size of the jitter falls off as $1/\sqrt{N}$.

(04-pressure-predict)=
## Predict before you calculate

Commit to an answer for each of these *before* running anything. Write them down. The point
is not to be right — it is to find out which of your intuitions the simulation will overturn.

1. A gas of $N$ particles at temperature $T$ sits in a box of volume $V$. You double $N$ at
   fixed $T$ and $V$. What happens to the pressure? What happens to the *relative* size of
   its fluctuations?
2. You double the mass $m$ of every particle while holding the temperature fixed. Each
   particle now carries more momentum per collision. Does the pressure go up?
3. Two boxes hold the same gas at the same temperature. One holds $100$ particles, the other
   $10^{6}$. Which one's pressure gauge visibly twitches?
4. At equilibrium, has the microscopic motion stopped?

:::{note} Why we ask first
Question 2 catches most people, and question 4 catches almost everyone at least once. A
prediction you have committed to is the only way to notice you were wrong — reading the
right answer afterwards feels like you knew it all along.
:::

:::{admonition} Model specification
:class: model-spec
- **System:** $N$ point particles of mass $m$ in a rectangular two-dimensional container.
- **Dynamics:** free flight between perfectly elastic collisions with the walls; particles do not interact with one another.
- **Boundary:** rigid fixed walls; a piston is represented by changing the box dimensions.
- **Ensemble:** approximately microcanonical — energy is exactly conserved, and the initial velocities are drawn from a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution at $T$.
- **Ignored:** intermolecular forces, particle size, quantum effects, gravity, and any mechanism that would let the gas thermalise on its own.
- **Valid when:** the gas is dilute and classical — mean free path much larger than the particle size, thermal de Broglie wavelength much smaller than the spacing.
- **Failure modes:** high density (particle–particle collisions matter), low temperature (quantum statistics), strong interactions (the ideal gas law itself fails).
:::

:::{admonition} What this model cannot do
:class: model-assumption
Because the particles never collide with *each other*, this model cannot **establish** a
Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution of speeds — it can only preserve one. That is why the
distribution is imposed at $t=0$. A model that derived the distribution would need
interparticle collisions, which is a different (and much slower) simulation. Be suspicious of
any textbook figure that shows speeds "relaxing" in a non-interacting gas.
:::

(04-pressure-derive)=
## Derive the result

We now do the calculation the simulation is imitating. Everything below is exact for the
model above; nothing is approximated except where it says so.

**System and boundary.** The gas inside the container. The wall is the boundary; we compute
the force it feels.

**Independent variables.** $N$, $V$, $T$, and the particle mass $m$.

**Sign convention.** Momentum transferred *to the wall* is counted positive.

### One particle, one wall

Take a wall perpendicular to the $x$ axis. A particle with velocity component $v_x$ hits it
and rebounds elastically, leaving with $-v_x$. The particle's momentum changed by
$-2 m v_x$, so by momentum conservation the wall received

$$
\Delta p = 2 m |v_x| .
$$

:::{admonition} Elastic collision with a wall
:class: model-assumption
"Elastic" here means the wall is infinitely massive and perfectly rigid, so the particle's
speed is unchanged and only the normal component reverses. A real wall recoils and vibrates —
that is how a gas and its container exchange energy and come to the same temperature.
:::

Between collisions with that same wall the particle must cross the box and come back, a
round trip of length $2L_x$, which takes

$$
\Delta t = \frac{2 L_x}{|v_x|} .
$$

So this one particle delivers momentum to that one wall at the average rate

$$
F_1 = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = \frac{2 m |v_x|}{2L_x / |v_x|} = \frac{m v_x^2}{L_x}.
$$

Note what happened: the awkward $|v_x|$ became $v_x^2$. The *direction* dropped out, and only
the energy-like quantity survived. That is the first hint that pressure will turn out to be
about energy, not about impacts.

### All the particles

Sum over the $N$ particles and divide by the wall's area $A = V/L_x$:

$$
P = \frac{1}{A}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{m v_{x,i}^2}{L_x}
  = \frac{m}{V}\sum_{i=1}^{N} v_{x,i}^2
  = \frac{N m \langle v_x^2 \rangle}{V},
$$

where $\langle v_x^2 \rangle$ is the average over particles. This is exact for the model.

### Bringing in temperature

So far no thermodynamics has entered — this is pure mechanics, and it would hold for any
distribution of speeds. Temperature enters through one additional statement:

:::{admonition} Equipartition of energy
:class: theorem
For a classical system in thermal equilibrium, each quadratic degree of freedom carries a
mean energy $\tfrac{1}{2} k_B T$. For translational motion,

$$
\tfrac{1}{2} m \langle v_x^2 \rangle = \tfrac{1}{2} k_B T
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
m \langle v_x^2 \rangle = k_B T .
$$

This is a theorem of classical statistical mechanics, not a definition, and it fails when
quantum effects freeze out a degree of freedom — which is exactly why the heat capacity of a
solid collapses at low temperature (module 16).
:::

Substituting:

$$
\boxed{\; P V = N k_B T \;}
$$

:::{admonition} What we have and have not shown
:class: approximation
We derived the ideal gas law from mechanics **plus** equipartition, for a gas of
non-interacting point particles. We did not derive it for real gases: at high density the
particles' own volume and their mutual attraction both matter, and the law acquires
corrections (module 14). The ideal gas law is an *empirical law* historically, and a
*theorem* only within this model.
:::

Notice that the mass cancelled. Doubling $m$ at fixed $T$ makes each particle slower —
$\langle v_x^2\rangle = k_B T/m$ — by exactly enough to offset its heavier punch. If you
predicted that heavier particles push harder, this is the sentence to reread.

(04-pressure-verify)=
## Verify computationally

Deriving a formula and trusting it are different things. The laboratory checks the result
four ways, and each check is also a test in the project's test suite, so the claims on this
page cannot silently rot.

**1. The equation of state.** Measure pressure from the summed wall impulses,
$P = \sum \Delta p / (t \cdot \text{wall})$, and compare with $N k_B T/V$. They agree within
statistical error, across eight independent random seeds.

**2. Energy conservation.** Elastic reflection flips a velocity component's sign, so the
total kinetic energy must be conserved to machine precision — not approximately. If it
drifts, the simulation is wrong, not the physics.

**3. Equipartition.** The measured mean kinetic energy per particle must equal
$\tfrac{d}{2} k_B T$ — in two dimensions, $k_B T$.

**4. Fluctuations.** This is the answer to the opening puzzle.

:::{admonition} Where the steadiness comes from
:class: numerical-observation
The time-averaged pressure of a two-dimensional ideal gas is proportional to the sum of $2N$
squared velocity components. A sum of $2N$ independent squared Gaussians is chi-square
distributed, whose relative standard deviation is $\sqrt{2/(2N)} = N^{-1/2}$. So

$$
\frac{\sigma_P}{\langle P \rangle} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} .
$$

Measured across independently drawn microstates, the fitted exponent is $-0.5$ and the
coefficient is $1$. The pressure of $100$ particles wobbles by about $10\%$; for a mole,
$N^{-1/2} \approx 4 \times 10^{-13}$ — undetectable by any instrument ever built.
:::

That is the resolution. The collisions never became gentle. There are simply so many of them
that the fractional jitter fell below the noise floor of reality.

:::{admonition} A caution about what the simulation proves
:class: open-question
Numerical agreement supports the derivation; it does not establish it. Our particles never
collide with each other, so the simulation cannot tell us whether a *real* gas reaches the
Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution — only that *if* it has one, the pressure follows. Keep the
two claims separate.
:::

(04-pressure-transfer)=
## Transfer the idea

The same reasoning — a steady macroscopic quantity as an average over many random microscopic
events, with $N^{-1/2}$ fluctuations — reappears throughout the course and beyond it.

- **Osmotic pressure.** Dissolved solute molecules bombard a membrane exactly as gas molecules
  bombard a wall, giving $\Pi V = N k_B T$: the same law, a different physical setting.
- **Radiation pressure.** Photons carry momentum $E/c$ rather than $mv$, and the derivation
  runs the same way, giving $P = u/3$ for isotropic radiation (module 16).
- **Shot noise.** An electric current is a stream of discrete electrons; its relative
  fluctuation also falls as $N^{-1/2}$, which is why a small photodetector current looks
  noisy and a large one does not.
- **Polling.** A sample of $N$ voters estimates a population fraction with an uncertainty of
  order $N^{-1/2}$ — the reason a poll of 1000 people has a margin near $3\%$, regardless of
  the size of the country.

:::{admonition} A word on "macroscopic"
:class: definition
A quantity is **macroscopic** when its relative fluctuation is negligible for the system size
at hand. That makes "macroscopic" a statement about $N$, not about size in metres: a
nanodroplet with $10^{9}$ molecules has a perfectly well-defined pressure, while ten
molecules in a trap do not.
:::

(04-pressure-advanced)=
## Advanced: beyond the simplest picture

:::{admonition} Advanced — safe to skip on a first pass
:class: advanced
Nothing later in the core course depends on this section.
:::

**Why interparticle collisions do not change the pressure.** In the derivation each particle
delivers $m v_x^2 / L_x$ to the wall. Collisions between particles redistribute velocities but
conserve total momentum and energy, so the *sum* $\sum m v_{x,i}^2$ is unchanged over times
long compared with a collision. The pressure depends only on that sum. This is why the ideal
gas law survives in a gas that collides constantly — and it is also why the argument stops
working the moment the collisions are not instantaneous or the particles feel each other at a
distance, since then there is a potential-energy contribution to the momentum flux.

**Pressure as momentum flux.** The sharper statement is that pressure is the flux of $x$
momentum in the $x$ direction, a component of the stress tensor
$\Pi_{ij} = \sum_i m v_i v_j / V$. Written this way there is no need for a wall at all:
pressure exists in the interior of a fluid, where there is nothing to bounce off. Walls are a
pedagogical crutch, and the crutch is worth putting down.

**The virial correction.** For interacting particles the exact result is the virial equation

$$
P V = N k_B T + \frac{1}{d}\left\langle \sum_{i<j} \mathbf{r}_{ij} \cdot \mathbf{F}_{ij} \right\rangle ,
$$

where the second term collects the interparticle forces. Attractive forces make it negative
and reduce the pressure below the ideal value — the physical origin of the $a$ term in the
van der Waals equation (module 14).

**Fluctuations and the ensemble.** The $N^{-1/2}$ result above compares independently drawn
microstates at fixed $T$, which is a canonical statement. If instead the energy is held
exactly fixed (microcanonical), the pressure fluctuations differ in detail but still vanish as
$N^{-1/2}$. The agreement of ensembles in the thermodynamic limit is a theorem worth meeting
properly in module 11 — and it is why we can be casual about which one a real experiment
realises.

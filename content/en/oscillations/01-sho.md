# The simple harmonic oscillator

(01-sho-puzzle)=
## The puzzle: the clock that ignores how hard you push it

Around 1602, Galileo timed the swinging bronze lamp in the cathedral of Pisa against his own
pulse and noticed something absurd: as the swing died down from wide arcs to small ones, the
*time per swing did not change*. Push a child's swing hard or gently — the round trip takes
the same time. Pendulum clocks kept civilisation's time for three centuries on exactly this
absurdity.

It gets stranger. Hang a mass on a spring: same behaviour. The vibration of a CO$_2$
molecule, the sloshing of charge in an LC circuit, a tuning fork, a building swaying in
wind — one curve, $\cos$, everywhere, with a period that refuses to care how big the motion
is.

:::{important} The question
Why does the period of an oscillator not depend on its amplitude? A wider swing has farther
to travel — why doesn't it take longer? And why does *every* small vibration in nature,
whatever its mechanism, follow the same cosine?
:::

The answer to the second question is one Taylor expansion, and it is the reason this module
— the smallest physical system in the course — is the foundation for everything up to and
including the laser.

3. Where in the cycle is the mass moving fastest — at the ends of its swing, at the centre,
   or somewhere in between? Where is it accelerating hardest?
4. Over one cycle, the total mechanical energy: constant, oscillating at the period, or
   oscillating at *twice* the period?

:::{note} Why we ask first
Question 1 is Galileo's absurdity and most people's intuition votes "longer". Question 4 is
subtler than it looks: the *total* is constant, but each of its two halves oscillates — and
at a frequency that surprises.
:::

:::{admonition} Model specification
:class: model-spec
- **System:** a point mass $m$ on a massless linear spring of stiffness $k$, moving in one dimension; the observables are position, velocity and the energies.
- **Dynamics:** Newton's second law with the Hooke restoring force, $m\ddot{x} = -kx$, integrated numerically by a velocity-Verlet scheme with a fixed time step.
- **Boundary:** none — the mass moves on an infinite line and nothing is exchanged with anything.
- **Ensemble:** a single deterministic trajectory per choice of $(x_0, v_0)$; where measurement noise is added it is Gaussian, independent, and seeded.
- **Ignored:** spring mass, nonlinearity at large extension, internal damping, and the three-dimensional world the real spring hangs in.
- **Valid when:** displacements stay in the linear regime of the real spring and the time step is well below the oscillation period.
- **Failure modes:** amplitudes large enough to feel the spring's nonlinearity, time steps near or above the stability limit, and any question about where the oscillation's energy ultimately goes.
:::

(01-sho-derive)=
## Derive the result

**System.** A mass $m$ on a spring of stiffness $k$; $x$ measures displacement from
equilibrium.

**The equation of motion.** Hooke's law gives the restoring force $F = -kx$, so Newton's
second law reads

$$
m\ddot{x} = -kx
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\ddot{x} = -\wnat^2\, x,
\qquad
\wnat \equiv \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} .
$$

:::{admonition} Hooke's law
:class: empirical-law
The linearity $F = -kx$ is a statement about real springs, true for small extensions and
increasingly false for large ones. Within this module it is taken as exact; the advanced
section and the pendulum below show what its failure looks like.
:::

**The solution.** The unique solution with initial position $x_0$ and initial velocity
$v_0$ is

$$
x(t) = A\cos(\wnat t + \varphi),
\qquad
A = \sqrt{x_0^2 + (v_0/\wnat)^2},
\qquad
\varphi = \operatorname{atan2}(-v_0/\wnat,\; x_0),
$$

as you can check by substitution — and, in phasor language, this is the arrow of module 00
with length $A$ and angle $-\varphi$, rotating at $\wnat$.

**The isochronism.** Everything the puzzle asked is now visible in one place: the angular
frequency $\wnat = \sqrt{k/m}$ contains the mass and the stiffness — *and not the
amplitude*. Doubling the release amplitude doubles the distance the mass must cover, but the
restoring force at every stage of the journey is also doubled, and the two effects cancel
exactly. That cancellation is special to the *linear* force law: it is not a property of
oscillation in general, but of $F \propto -x$.

**Velocity and the quarter-cycle lag.** Differentiating,
$v(t) = -A\wnat\sin(\wnat t + \varphi)$: the speed is largest as the mass crosses the
centre, zero at the turning points — velocity runs a quarter cycle ahead of position.

**Energy.** The kinetic and potential energies are

$$
K = \tfrac12 m v^2 = \tfrac12 k A^2 \sin^2(\wnat t + \varphi),
\qquad
U = \tfrac12 k x^2 = \tfrac12 k A^2 \cos^2(\wnat t + \varphi),
$$

whose sum is constant, $E = \tfrac12 k A^2$, while each half oscillates — note, via
$\sin^2$ and $\cos^2$ — at *twice* the natural frequency: the kinetic energy peaks twice per
swing, once for each crossing of the centre. In phase space the constant energy is the
ellipse $\wnat^2 x^2 + v^2 = \wnat^2 A^2$.

**Universality.** Why is this one system everywhere? Take *any* potential $U(x)$ with a
stable equilibrium at $x_e$ and Taylor-expand:

$$
U(x) = U(x_e) + \tfrac12 U''(x_e)\,(x - x_e)^2 + \mathcal{O}\big((x-x_e)^3\big).
$$

The linear term vanishes because $x_e$ is an equilibrium; the constant is irrelevant; and
what remains, close enough to $x_e$, is a spring with $k = U''(x_e)$.

:::{admonition} Small oscillations are harmonic
:class: approximation
Near any stable equilibrium with $U''(x_e) > 0$, the motion is simple harmonic with
$\wnat = \sqrt{U''(x_e)/m}$, up to corrections controlled by the cubic term — valid while
the amplitude stays small against the scale on which $U''$ changes. This is why molecules,
bridges, circuits and cathedral lamps all play the same note: not because nature loves
springs, but because every smooth valley is a parabola at its bottom.
:::

(01-sho-verify)=
## Verify computationally

Deriving a formula and trusting it are different things. The laboratory checks the results
numerically, and each check is also a test in the project's test suite, so the claims on
this page cannot silently rot.

**1. The closed form.** The velocity-Verlet integration of $m\ddot{x} = -kx$ against
$A\cos(\wnat t + \varphi)$: at a thousand steps per period the two agree to better than one
part in $10^3$ over twenty periods.

**2. Energy conservation.** Over one hundred periods the integrator's energy error stays
*bounded*, oscillating with relative amplitude $(\wnat\,\Delta t)^2/4$ — it does not drift.

:::{admonition} A bounded error is a designed property
:class: numerical-observation
The measured energy wobble at one hundred steps per period is $9.9 \times 10^{-4}$ of the
total, and the mean energy of the first and last periods agree to better than $10^{-7}$.
This is a property of the *symplectic* integrator, not of integrators in general: the naive
Euler scheme at the same step size spirals outward visibly within a few periods. The
laboratory lets you watch it do so.
:::

**3. Isochronism, measured.** Fit the period at release amplitudes spanning a factor of ten:
the fitted periods agree within the fit uncertainty.

**4. A noisy measurement.** The laboratory's final experiment treats the simulation as
apparatus: Gaussian noise is added to the trajectory, a cosine is fitted, and the natural
frequency is recovered as $\wnat_{\text{fit}} \pm \sigma$ — agreeing with $\sqrt{k/m}$
within three standard errors, across eight independent seeds. Not
$\wnat = 4.000000$: $\wnat = 4.001 \pm 0.001$. That habit is the course's measurement
culture, and it starts here.

(01-sho-transfer)=
## Transfer the idea

- **The pendulum.** For small angles, $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ turns the pendulum
  equation into an oscillator equation with $\wnat = \sqrt{g/\ell}$ — no mass, which is
  its own famous surprise.
- **The LC circuit.** Charge on a capacitor discharging through an inductor obeys
  $L\ddot{q} = -q/C$: the same equation with $m \to L$, $k \to 1/C$. Electrical resonance is
  this module with the symbols renamed.
- **Molecular vibrations.** The bond of CO$_2$ near its equilibrium length is a valley;
  $U''$ at its bottom sets infrared absorption frequencies — how astronomers identify
  molecules they will never touch.
- **Phonons and the quantum oscillator.** Crystals are lattices of coupled oscillators
  (module 02 makes this literal), and the quantum harmonic oscillator — energy levels
  $\hbar\wnat(n + \tfrac12)$ — inherits every structure built here.

:::{admonition} The natural frequency
:class: definition
The **natural frequency** $\wnat$ of a system is the angular frequency at which it
oscillates when displaced and released, with no driving and no damping. It is a property of
the system — of $m$ and $k$ — not of the motion: amplitude and phase belong to the initial
conditions, $\wnat$ does not.
:::

(01-sho-advanced)=
## Advanced: when the parabola fails

:::{admonition} Advanced — safe to skip on a first pass
:class: advanced
Nothing later in the core course depends on this section.
:::

**The pendulum's confession.** The exact pendulum period grows with amplitude:

$$
T(\theta_0) = T_{\text{small}}
\left(1 + \frac{\theta_0^2}{16} + \frac{11\,\theta_0^4}{3072} + \cdots\right).
$$

At $\theta_0 = 20°$ the correction is under $1\%$ — Galileo's pulse stood no chance — but at
$90°$ the period is longer by about $18\%$. Isochronism was always an approximation; the
laboratory's final exploration integrates the exact pendulum and lets you watch the
approximation die. Historical footnote: Huygens knew, and built cycloidal cheeks into his
clocks to make the period *exactly* amplitude-free — the first perturbation correction in
engineering history.

**Anharmonicity as a frontier.** Keep the cubic term the Taylor expansion discarded and the
oscillation's frequency begins to depend on amplitude, harmonics of $\wnat$ appear in the
motion, and neighbouring oscillators can exchange energy in new ways. That is not a defect:
thermal expansion of solids, the workings of an optical frequency comb, and the entire
subject of nonlinear optics (module 20) live in those discarded terms.

**Two conserved pictures.** The energy ellipse of this module is the $N = 1$ case of a deep
pattern: phase-space orbits of conservative systems foliate into nested invariant curves.
When modules 02–03 couple many oscillators, the ellipse becomes a torus and the normal-mode
transformation is the change of coordinates that untangles it — the classical shadow of what
quantum mechanics will call diagonalising the Hamiltonian.
